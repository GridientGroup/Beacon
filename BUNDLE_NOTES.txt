NEW IN 115 — THIS ONE IS LIVE, AND IT IS THE WORST KIND FOR THIS PRODUCT
══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

**One of your customers is being shown an estimated ENERGY STAR score for a
building type EPA does not score at all.**

Here is the chain. The building type used by all the math comes from
`customers.type`. I read what that column actually contains in production:

    Healthcare · Hospitality · Manufacturing · office · Retail ·
    Supermarket · Warehouse

Those are capitalised display words. The math indexes by lowercase slugs. Six
of the seven lowercase straight onto a valid slug and are fine. One does not:

    'Manufacturing' -> 'manufacturing' -> not a slug at all.

The correct slug is `industrial`, whose own display label is literally
"Manufacturing/Industrial".

By itself that would be survivable — an unknown type falls back to a median
EUI of 80, and industrial's median IS 80, so the EUI happens to come out
right. The ENERGY STAR tile is where it turns into a real problem, because
eligibility was asked like this:

    var essEligible = window.BTYPE_ESS_ELIGIBLE[btype] !== false;

That **fails open**. An unrecognised slug gives `undefined`, and
`undefined !== false` is `true`. So the building is treated as ENERGY STAR
eligible and an estimated score is calculated and displayed.

Measured, on an identical building:

    btype 'Manufacturing' (as stored)  ->  eligible: true   score shown: 67
    btype 'industrial'    (correct)    ->  eligible: false  score shown: none

EPA does not certify manufacturing plants. The table in benchmarks.js already
knew that — `industrial: false` has been sitting there correctly the whole
time. The lookup simply never reached it.

Of everything found in this codebase so far, this is the one I would least
want in front of a client. An EUI that is 10% off is a bad estimate. A
number labelled ENERGY STAR, for a property class the program does not
cover, is a different category of wrong.

FIXED TWO WAYS, because either one alone would leave the door open:

  1. **Normalise the type once, at the boundary.** `beaconNormalizeBtype()`
     maps every spelling that actually reaches us onto a canonical slug —
     the production `customers.type` values, the EPA/ENERGY STAR property-type
     display labels (which are the only building-type vocabulary in the

1. REPORTED SQUARE FOOTAGE ON A SIBLING METER WAS THROWN AWAY.

   A building with an electric and a gas meter is two rows in the portfolio.
   The sqft estimator was only ever shown the FIRST row. If that row's Square
   Feet cell was blank — very often the gas row — the real floor area sitting
   on the other row was ignored and the building was modelled from CBECS
   medians instead.

   That estimate's own stated confidence band is 0.70x to 1.50x. Everything
   downstream rides on it: EUI, $/sqft, kg CO2/sqft, solar array sizing, and
   the 25,000-sqft threshold that decides whether a building is even in scope
   for a Building Performance Standard. A 42,000 sqft building was being
   modelled at 63,385 — which is the difference between "covered by the
   ordinance" and "not covered."

   The building-level maximum was already being computed twelve lines above.
   It is now handed to the estimator, at both call sites (map popup and
   Location page), so "reported wins, always" holds per BUILDING rather than
   per first-row.

2. THE CONTRACTED SUPPLY RATE NEVER DISPLAYED.

   Both ingest paths collect a real per-account supply rate — the Supabase
   loader from rate_cents / supply_rate_cents, the upload parser from the
   "Supply Rate (c/kWh)" column. The Location page has a branch that upgrades
   the identity grid from "STATE AVG RATE" to a true "your rate vs state avg
   (+/-%)" comparison whenever that rate is present.

   The object passed into that renderer never carried the field. The branch
   could not fire. Every location displayed the state average — including the
   ones where we hold the signed contract. A client locked at 8.9c in a 12.4c
   state was shown "12.4c/kWh - Deregulated", which is not their rate, and
   worse, buries the fact that their procurement is working.

   Rate is c/kWh, so it is taken from the building's largest ELECTRIC meter;
   a gas row's rate column is $/therm and must never be printed as cents.

   Two smaller things surfaced alongside it:

     - The guard on that whole row was `accountType !== 'Gas'` — an exact,
       case-sensitive match against one spelling. The loader emits lowercase
       'gas', so gas-only buildings sailed past it and were shown a state
       average ELECTRIC rate, in a unit they have no meter in.

     - Rate parsing stripped every character that wasn't a digit or a dot,
       which removes a minus sign. A mis-keyed -8.9 became +8.9, cleared the
       "greater than zero" sanity check, and shipped as a real contracted
       rate. Fixed in all three places that parse a rate.

3. THE CLIMATE ARGUMENT WAS ACCEPTED AND NEVER READ.

   The Location page passes the building's city into computeBenchmarks, and
   the comment at that call site says it lets the function "pick the ASHRAE
   climate-zone-aware EUI adjustment instead of the coarser state-level
   bucket."

   The function never touched the argument. The median EUI was adjusted by a
   single per-state number, so San Diego and Redding, Miami and Tallahassee,
   Seattle and Spokane were all held to the same expectation. The comment
   described behaviour that did not exist — which is worse than the coarse
   bucket, because it invites trust in a precision the number never had.

   This was implemented rather than deleted, because the data to do it
   properly already ships in the bundle. weather_norm.js resolves a city to
   its NOAA station and ASHRAE 169 zone; reference_buildings.js holds DOE
   prototype total EUI per zone. When a building's city zone differs from its
   state's default zone, the median is scaled by the ratio of the DOE
   reference totals for those two zones — same dataset, same building type,
   only the climate differs, so the ratio isolates the climate effect alone.

   Deliberately conservative. It collapses to exactly the old behaviour when
   the city resolves to the state's own zone, when no station matches, or
   when the building type has no DOE prototype, and it is clamped to
   0.75-1.35 because a correction outside that band means the lookup went
   wrong, not that the building is somewhere extraordinary.

   One trap worth recording, because it is the same bug this fix exists to
   remove: the station lookup silently falls back to the STATE's principal
   station when a city name isn't in the index, and still returns a zone.
   Taking that zone would have handed a Tallahassee building Miami's 1A and
   labelled it zone-precise — a state-level number wearing a city badge. The
   correction now requires a genuine city match.

   Benchmarks return climateBasis ('city_zone' or 'state'), climateZone and
   climateAdj, so a panel can state which basis it used rather than implying
   zone precision on a state-level number.

   Verified: San Francisco office median 43 vs Los Angeles 48 (same state);
   Tallahassee, unknown cities, unknown building types and missing states all
   collapse to adj 1.000 / basis 'state'; the building's ACTUAL EUI does not
   move with city — climate adjusts the expectation, never the meter.

000000000. THE EUI TILE WAS THROWING AWAY GAS.  ** NEW IN 107 **

   Reference Building Comparison opened its loop with:

       if (acctType(acct) !== 'electric') return;

   So a building's GAS meter was skipped entirely, while its ELECTRIC meter
   supplied both the consumption and the floor area. EUI is total SITE energy
   per square foot. Dropping a fuel understates it, and it understates in one
   direction only — so every building with gas looked more efficient than it
   actually is, and the tile has been flattering them since it shipped.

   Measured on the live portfolio: mean EUI 57.4 electric-only against 64.4
   all-fuel. A 12.3% understatement across the whole portfolio, and far worse
   on the 110 buildings that actually have gas.

   On a heating-dominated building it is not a rounding error. Same school,
   same data, before and after:

       electric only          68.2 kBtu/sqft   -13.1%   "Below reference"
       with its own gas meter 128.2 kBtu/sqft   +63.4%   "Investigate"

   A complete verdict reversal. Anyone shown the old number was told a
   gas-heated building was beating its class when it was overrunning it.

   FIXED, and the fix is structural rather than a patched conditional:
   meters are now aggregated into BUILDINGS before anything is computed.
   All fuels are converted to kBtu (electric x 3.412, gas x 100) and summed;
   floor area is taken as the MAX across a building's meters, never the sum,
   because a two-meter site would otherwise double its own area and halve its
   EUI. Buildings are keyed on location_id, falling back to the store-code
   convention (VAN-MO060 pairs with VAN-MO060-G) when the upload path has not
   supplied one.

000000000b. EXCLUDED BUILDINGS WERE SILENT.  ** NEW IN 107 **

   The same tile dropped any building it could not compare with a bare
   `return` and never counted it — then printed a confident portfolio verdict
   over whatever subset happened to survive.

   The biggest cause is not a bug in Beacon, it is a real gap in the source
   data. DOE publishes medium_office for all 15 climate zones, but every
   other prototype for only 2A / 4A / 5A (+6A for some). A warehouse in
   California (3B) or a supermarket in Georgia (3A) has NO reference to
   compare against and simply vanished. 62 of the 311 live locations sit in
   such a zone.

   The tile now counts and NAMES every exclusion, with its reason:

       Buildings compared            1 of 3
       Excluded - no DOE prototype   2 (Warehouse in zone 3B, Supermarket in zone 3A)
       Excluded - no property type   1

   and when coverage drops below 90% the source line says so outright:
   "This verdict covers 33% of the portfolio - the excluded buildings are
   listed above and are not represented in the figure."

   A verdict over 60% of a portfolio is a different claim from a verdict over
   all of it, and the tile no longer lets those two look alike.

0000000000000. EUI WAS BACK-SOLVED FROM DOLLARS, EVEN WITH METERS ON FILE.
                                                        ** NEW IN 112 **

   computeBenchmarks derives energy from SPEND. That is a fair fallback when
   only spend is known — but it was being used by callers that had the real
   metered kWh and therms sitting in a variable a few lines above.

   Converting the whole bill at the ELECTRIC rate values a therm at

       true site energy     8,412,000 kBtu   EUI 84.1
       back-solved          7,100,000 kBtu   EUI 71.0   (-15.6%)
       with real meters                      EUI 84.0   (exact)

   Heating-dominated buildings ran far worse. And because the shortfall is
   multiplicative, such buildings landed below 0.85 x median and printed a
   green "Better than median" verdict essentially by construction.
   perloc.js has always done this correctly — which is why the Portfolio Map
   and the Location page showed DIFFERENT EUIs FOR THE SAME BUILDING.

   Two more defects in the same function, same root:

   · TWO FUEL SPLITS, THIRTY LINES APART. The EUI line treated 100% of spend

0000000. WEATHER-NORMALIZED SAVINGS WERE OVERSTATED ~5x.  ** NEW IN 105 **

   File: weather_normalization.js — normalizedAnnual()

   It was one line:

       return predict(model, normalHDD, normalCDD);

   The regression is fit on BILLING PERIODS, so its intercept is the baseload
   for ONE month. normalHDD and normalCDD are ANNUAL degree-day totals. That
   line therefore added one month of baseload to a year of weather-driven
   load and threw away the other eleven — which is most of the consumption
   that does NOT change with weather.

   It does not simply scale the answer down. Baseline and reporting
   regressions divide load between intercept and slopes differently, so
   dropping most of the intercept corrupts the two figures by different
   amounts, and the savings percentage computed from them is meaningless.

   Measured on a demo account with an 8% gain planted in it:

       old form:   baseline  791,271   reporting  436,452   -> "44.8% saved"
       corrected:  baseline 1,512,178  reporting 1,388,519  -> "8.2% saved"
       actual metered 12-month sums:
                            1,511,743             1,388,258

   The corrected form lands within 0.03% of the real totals and recovers the
   planted gain. Across four scenarios it now returns 5.5%, 7.4%, 8.2% and
   8.3% — all inside the 6-13% band that was planted.

   The fix annualizes by the number of billing periods, and that count is now
   INFERRED from the bill dates (median period length, snapped to 12/6/4)
   rather than assumed, so a bimonthly or quarterly commercial account
   annualizes correctly too.

   Nothing in the app consumed this function before today, so no previously
   displayed number was wrong. That is also exactly why it survived: the
   module was built, loaded, and never run.

000000b. THE ANOMALY DETECTOR WAS FLAGGING WINTER.  ** NEW IN 105 **

   File: bill_anomaly.js — detectAnomalies()

   It computed a weather-adjusted prediction for every month and then never
   used it. The z-score was taken on RAW consumption against a rolling window
   of RAW consumption. In any building with a heating or cooling load,
   January is legitimately far from the annual mean, so seasonality itself
   scored as anomalous.

   On four test meters, each with one excursion planted:
       old: 2-5 false positives per meter, planted excursion MISSED in 3 of 4
       new: 0 false positives, planted excursion FOUND in 4 of 4 (z 4.2-7.8)

   Two changes:

   · WHAT GETS SCORED. When a valid degree-day model exists, the detector now
     scores the RESIDUAL (actual minus weather-predicted) — what is left once
     weather is accounted for: equipment faults, schedule changes, occupancy
     shifts, billing errors. With no model it falls back to raw consumption,
     and every result carries a `basis` field saying which path produced it,
     so a report can never imply a weather-adjusted finding it did not make.

   · TWO GATES INSTEAD OF ONE. A flag now needs both:
        |z| > 2.0   (was 1.5, which flags ~13% of all periods by chance —
                     more than one month a year on a building where nothing
                     happened; 2.0 is ~4.6%)
        >= 10% off the weather-adjusted expectation

     The second gate is the one that matters in practice. A z-score is
     scale-free, so on a building with tight residuals a month that is 3% off
     scores z=2.2 and gets reported. Statistically true, operationally
     useless — nobody opens a work order over a 3% bill variance.

     Neither number comes from a standard. IPMVP and ASHRAE G14 cover savings
     determination, not exception-report tuning; 10% is the common utility
     bill-audit convention. Both are overridable per call and both travel on
     every result, so the tile states the screen it applied on its face.

0000. M&V STATISTICS WERE WRONG IN THREE WAYS.  ** NEW IN 102 **

   weather_normalization.js is the module that decides whether a savings claim
   is statistically defensible. It had three defects:

     1. It advertised "ASHRAE Guideline 14: R2 > 0.75, CV(RMSE) < 20%".
        The real G14 monthly criteria are CV(RMSE) <= 15% and |NMBE| <= 5%.
     2. CV(RMSE) divided by n instead of (n - p). On 12 bills with 3 fitted
        parameters that understated the error by a factor of sqrt(12/9) —
        about 15%.
     3. NMBE was not computed at all, so one of the two real criteria was
        simply absent.

   Also corrected: the R2 >= 0.75 threshold is NOT from Guideline 14. It comes
   from the Bonneville Power Administration "Regression for M&V Reference
   Guide" section 5.1.1, and is now attributed there.

   The usual G14 clause numbers are marked [UNVERIFIED] in the source — the
   standard is paywalled, the threshold VALUES are corroborated independently,
   the clause references are not. Nothing prints a clause number to a client.

   ADDED: leave-one-out CV(RMSE). This matters more than it sounds. In-sample
   NMBE is ~0 BY CONSTRUCTION for any least-squares fit with an intercept —
   measured at 1e-14 or smaller across every test case — so on the baseline
   period it passes every time and validates nothing. It is kept because it is
   a required reporting statistic and becomes meaningful in the reporting
   period, but loo_cv_rmse is now the honest out-of-sample number. It is
   computed from the closed-form hat-matrix leverage, so it costs one extra
   pass rather than refitting the model n times.

   VERIFIED against an independent numpy implementation on an identical
   24-month fixture: intercept, both slopes, R2, CV(RMSE), NMBE and
   leave-one-out CV(RMSE) all agree to roughly 14 significant figures.

   ⚠️ BEHAVIOUR CHANGE. The threshold drop and the (n - p) correction compound.
   A 12-month fit whose corrected CV(RMSE) lands between 15% and about 17.3%
   flips from PASS to FAIL. ANY SAVINGS CLAIM PREVIOUSLY REPORTED AS MEETING
   GUIDELINE 14 MUST BE RE-RUN BEFORE IT IS QUOTED AGAIN.

   fitModel() now also returns a `criteria` object with the value, threshold,
   pass flag and source for each of the three tests, so a report can say which
   test failed rather than only that the fit was rejected.

  1. UTILITY PRICE, FOR COMPARISON AND FOR DOLLARS.  [added 2026-09-17]
     Right now every analytic output is in physical units. The M&V tile says
     "278,464 kWh saved", not "$X saved", and there is no rate comparison
     anywhere. Two related but separate questions:
       a) can we price a portfolio's savings and overages in dollars
       b) can we compare what a client PAYS against what they could pay
     Beacon already ships openei.js (NREL Utility Rate Database) and
     market_intelligence.js, and there is a commodity-fetcher edge function
     with 638,177 rows of price history behind it. So some of the pieces are
     in hand. Worth a proper look at what each source can and cannot
     legitimately support before anything gets wired — a blended average rate
     applied to a demand-driven bill is the kind of number that looks
     authoritative and is wrong.

  2. THE DEMO PORTFOLIO'S USAGE DOES NOT MATCH ITS BUILDING TYPES.
     [needs your decision]

     Giving the demo accounts real property types exposed this. The demo
     usage figures were generated with no regard to building type, so every
     type sits in the same narrow 36-47 kBtu/sqft band:

       type                          accounts   demo EUI   DOE ref   verdict
       Office                             245       38.5      50.4    -23.7%
       Warehouse (Non-Refrigerated)        79       42.7      25.5    +67.5%
       Retail Store                        50       38.6      82.1    -53.0%
       Medical Office                      28       35.8     178.5    -80.0%
       K-12 School                         12       42.4      61.8    -31.4%
       Supermarket/Grocery Store            2       44.6     232.5    -80.8%
       Hotel                                2       46.1      74.5    -38.2%

     Only Office is credible. A real supermarket runs about 230 kBtu/sqft and
     a real warehouse about 25, so the demo currently shows a supermarket
     outperforming its class by 81% and a warehouse overrunning by 68%. The
     tile arithmetic is exact — it reproduces to the decimal — the INPUTS are
     implausible.

     This is also why the old "-24.7% STRONG OUTPERFORM" looked believable
     before 103: the demo band happens to sit near office EUI, so defaulting
     everything to Office hid incoherent demo data behind a plausible number.

     Two ways out, and it is your call because one of them changes numbers
     you have already shared:
       a) Re-seed the demo accounts' annual_usage so each building's EUI is
          realistic for its type. The demo then holds up to an expert
          audience. Cost: the portfolio's headline usage and carbon totals
          change, so Beacon_Capabilities_for_Reports_Engine.docx needs a
          revision. Vandelay is 62% Office and already close, so the shift is
          smaller than it sounds.
       b) Leave it. Nothing breaks, but the Reference Building tile shows
          verdicts an energy professional will not believe.
     I would do (a) — the tile's whole value is the verdict, and a verdict
     nobody believes is worth less than no verdict.
